1. Executive Summary
The Temple of Eka-Danta is a story-driven adventure set around an abandoned ancient temple
discovered at night. The player initially believes they are investigating a forgotten ruin. As the temple
awakens, murals, carvings and objects transport the player into mythological memory sequences
inspired by traditional stories associated with Ganesha.
The key design idea is a two-layer experience: Present-Day Mystery and Mythological Memory.
Exploration in the present uncovers a story; memories turn that story into playable adventure, puzzles
and boss encounters.
Core fantasy: “I am exploring a forgotten temple that remembers.”
Design goal: make the player feel curiosity first, wonder second, tension third, and leave them with a
sense of discovery rather than simply completing levels.

3. Story Premise
The player arrives at an abandoned temple deep inside a forest. It is nearly midnight. The structure is
covered in moss, roots and centuries of dust. Moonlight enters through broken sections of the roof.
Inside, the player discovers that every major chamber contains evidence of an old story: murals,
damaged inscriptions, ritual objects, statues and symbols. When certain objects are activated, the
temple briefly becomes a living memory.
The player is transported into a mythological episode inspired by traditional Ganesha narratives. These
episodes are not presented as literal historical recordings; the temple’s memory is a fictional
storytelling device.
After completing a memory, the player returns to the present. Something in the temple has changed: a
statue moves, a sealed door opens, a new inscription becomes visible, or a previously harmless object
becomes a clue.
Gradually, the player realizes the memories are connected. Something has been corrupting the
temple’s ability to preserve them.

4. Context & Worldbuilding
The Temple
The temple is an original fictional location inspired by South Indian and broader Indian temple
architecture. It should feel ancient without claiming to reproduce one specific real-world temple.
Why is it abandoned?
The game deliberately keeps this ambiguous at first. The temple may have been forgotten, sealed, or
deliberately hidden. The truth is revealed through exploration.
Why does it remember?
This is the game’s supernatural premise: the temple preserves symbolic memories through its
architecture. Moonlight acts as the trigger that makes those memories playable.
Why at night?
Night provides visual contrast, isolation and a natural role for moonlight. It also allows the temple to
feel different whenever the player enters a new memory.
5. Player Experience
First 5 minutes: curiosity. The player learns movement and exploration while approaching the temple.
First 15 minutes: mystery. The player discovers that the temple responds to them.
First memory: wonder. The environment transforms and the player realizes this is more than an
exploration game.
First major encounter: tension. A mythological conflict becomes an interactive gameplay sequence.
Mid-game: investigation. The player begins connecting symbols and events between the present and
the memories.
Final act: revelation. The player understands why the temple was built and what has been corrupting
its memories.
6. Core Gameplay Loop
EXPLORE → OBSERVE → INTERACT → ENTER MEMORY → SOLVE / SURVIVE / FIGHT →
DISCOVER CLUE → RETURN → UNLOCK
• Explore temple rooms, courtyards, underground passages and hidden chambers.
• Inspect carvings, murals, statues, inscriptions and environmental clues.
• Trigger memories through moonlight, symbols, objects or shrine mechanisms.
• Play through a mythological episode as an interactive sequence.
• Complete a puzzle, chase, combat encounter or boss encounter.
• Return to the present with new information or a new ability.
• Use that information to open the next area.

8. Chapter I — The Broken Tusk
Traditional inspiration: A well-known Ganesha narrative describes a confrontation with Parashurama in
which Ganesha’s tusk is struck by Parashurama’s axe. The game adapts the encounter into an
interactive memory.
Present: The player finds a damaged mural showing a one-tusked Ganesha and a warrior holding an
axe.
Memory: The player becomes Ganesha and encounters Parashurama at a mountain gateway.
Gameplay: dodge, block, create barriers and read attack patterns. The encounter emphasizes restraint
rather than simply defeating an opponent.
Signature moment: the tusk breaks. Sound drops out. The falling tusk becomes a visual transition back
to the abandoned temple.
Reward: a previously sealed passage opens because the temple now recognizes the completed
memory.
9. Chapter II — Vakratunda & Matsarasura
Traditional inspiration: The Mudgala Purana includes Vakratunda, a form of Ganesha, confronting
Matsarasura, a demon associated with envy.
Game adaptation: the temple becomes infected by a dark distortion that creates false copies of rooms
and objects.
Boss design: the encounter has phases based on illusion, envy, rage and resolution. The boss should
feel supernatural rather than like a generic monster.
Gameplay: identify false objects, avoid environmental traps, use light to expose illusions, then confront
the source of the corruption.
Purpose: this chapter establishes that the temple’s memories are being actively distorted.
10. Chapter III — The Curse of the Moon

Traditional inspiration: Traditional Ganesha stories include a well-known episode involving Ganesha
and the Moon. Versions differ across sources.
Signature mechanic: moonlight changes the temple.
• Moonlit state: hidden symbols appear and safe routes open.
• Shadow state: different doors, traps and corrupted objects become visible.
• The player rotates mirrors, opens roof mechanisms and moves objects to redirect moonlight.
Design goal: make the moon more than a background object — it becomes a gameplay system.
11. Chapter IV — The Serpent Below
This is an original game chapter designed to connect the mythological memories into the temple’s
fictional mystery.
The player discovers a huge serpent carved around an underground shrine. When the shrine activates,
the carving appears to move and an enormous serpent begins pursuing the player.
Gameplay: navigation, environmental puzzles, collapsing corridors, water passages and timed
escapes.
Twist: the serpent is not the true antagonist. It is guarding a sealed chamber containing evidence
about the temple’s original purpose.
12. Chapter V — The Great Scribe
Traditional inspiration: The popular tradition of Ganesha serving as Vyasa’s scribe for the
Mahabharata.
Game adaptation: an underground library contains damaged inscriptions and fragmented symbolic
text.
Gameplay: arrange fragments, match symbols, reconstruct sequences and identify missing pieces.
Visual idea: the temple itself behaves like a manuscript. Walls, pillars and statues become pieces of
the puzzle.
13. Final Act — The Sealed Sanctum
After completing the major memories, the player discovers that all of them share one hidden symbol. It
appears in every chapter but is never explained.
The player enters the deepest chamber. A giant Ganesha statue stands inside, apparently untouched
by time.
The player discovers that the temple was designed to preserve symbolic memories — but something
has been feeding on those memories and changing them.
Final encounter: the player does not fight Ganesha. The antagonist is the fictional corrupting force. The
player uses knowledge gained from every previous chapter to restore the temple.
Ending: the corruption disappears. Moonlight fills the sanctum. The temple becomes quiet again. At
sunrise, the player sees the temple differently: it is no longer dead, but preserved.

15. Exploration System
• Free exploration within connected temple zones.
• Optional side chambers contain lore, symbols and visual storytelling.
• No need to place collectibles everywhere; important objects should feel meaningful.
• The environment itself acts as the map.
• Locked areas are opened through memories rather than arbitrary keys.
• Return visits should reveal changes, encouraging players to re-examine earlier areas.
16. Puzzle Design
Principle: puzzles should feel like they belong to the temple rather than feeling like unrelated
mini-games.
• Moonlight alignment puzzles
• Stone-symbol sequence puzzles
• Rangoli pattern reconstruction
• Bell / sound sequence puzzles
• Statue orientation puzzles
• Water-flow and channel puzzles
• Mural reconstruction
• Environmental observation puzzles
17. Combat & Boss Philosophy

Combat should be limited and meaningful. The game is primarily an exploration adventure, not a
combat-heavy action game.
• Enemies should appear because the story requires them, not to fill every corridor.
• Bosses should have mechanics tied to their narrative identity.
• Avoid turning sacred figures into disposable enemies.
• Use environmental interaction, timing, light and puzzle-solving alongside combat.
Goal: every major encounter should be memorable because of its story and environment, not merely
its difficulty.
18. Art Direction
Overall look: dark cinematic fantasy grounded in Indian architectural details.
• Moonlit stone surfaces
• Moss, roots, rain and dust
• Broken pillars and weathered carvings
• Warm diya light against cool moonlight
• Deep shadows and volumetric light
• Subtle particles in shafts of moonlight
• Ancient murals that animate only during memories
Color logic: cool blue/silver for the present temple, warm gold for divine memory moments, darker
high-contrast tones for corruption.
19. Audio Direction
• Wind through stone corridors
• Crickets and distant forest ambience
• Water dripping in underground chambers
• Occasional temple bells
• Footsteps that change according to surface
• Low drones during supernatural events
• Traditional-inspired instrumentation used carefully, not continuously
Silence is an important sound-design tool. The most important supernatural moments can become
quieter rather than louder.
20. UI / UX
The UI should be intentionally minimal to preserve immersion.
• Small interaction prompt only when necessary.

• No permanent minimap unless testing proves navigation needs it.
• No excessive floating quest markers.
• Health / stamina indicators appear only during relevant action sequences.
• Lore can be accessed through an optional journal.
• Chapter transitions should be cinematic rather than menu-heavy.
21. Progression System
Progression is primarily knowledge-based rather than equipment-based.
• Memory fragments reveal new clues.
• Completed memories unlock temple mechanisms.
• New environmental abilities can be introduced gradually.
• Optional lore increases understanding but does not block the main story.
• Completion of one chapter changes the physical temple.

24. Technical Concept
Core systems: player controller, interaction system, dialogue/lore system, puzzle framework,
memory-transition system, chapter manager, save/progression system, audio zones, lighting states
and boss state machines.
Important reusable system: Memory Portal / Memory Trigger. Each mural, statue or relic should be able
to trigger a structured sequence: present-state → transition → memory-state → completion →
present-state update.
This architecture makes it easier to add future mythology-inspired chapters without rewriting the
entire game.
25. Ethical & Cultural Design Guidelines
• Treat Ganesha and other sacred figures respectfully.
• Do not use sacred figures as comedic skins or disposable enemies.
• Clearly distinguish traditional inspiration from fictional game events.
• Research each story before adapting it.
• Where traditions have multiple versions, acknowledge that variation rather than presenting one
version as the only truth.
• Keep original fictional antagonists clearly separate from revered deities.
This approach lets the game be dramatic and adventurous while still being culturally considerate.
26. Elevator Pitch
“The Temple of Eka-Danta is a cinematic mythological adventure where a player
explores an abandoned temple under moonlight. Every mural and relic can awaken
a playable memory inspired by traditional stories of Ganesha. Solve ancient
puzzles, survive supernatural encounters and uncover why the temple was built to
remember — and what is trying to make it forget.”
27. Final Vision
The goal is not to make another mythology game where the player simply fights enemies and collects
objects.
The goal is to create an experience that feels like walking into a forgotten Indian legend. The player
should remember the sound of the temple, the moonlight on the stone, the moment the mural came
